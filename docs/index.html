The Racer’s Mind Free

Are your race results consistently below your ability?

How often do you fall into the mental trap? You mess up qualifying and end up lower in the grid than you should be. You make a mistake in the race and it snowballs into more mistakes? You get passed and the red mist descends? You focus on trying to catch the guy ahead and make MORE mistakes?

When this happens, your mind switches into “outcome mode”. You’re no longer in a flow state, executing - you’ve been snapped out and now you’re worried about what already happened or what might happen.

Have you noticed that in high-pressure races, once your brain switches into “outcome” mode, your mistakes multiply and your results are too often below your abilities?

For me, the answer was YES. All the time. The Racer’s Mind was built to help me stay in that flow state and execute and I think it can help you too.

The idea

Your pace is built in practice. Your practice writes your “program” in your mind for how you drive that combo. Your pace is determined by running your program...every corner. Every lap. Monotonously. You can’t “will” the car to go faster. Fast is a consequence of doing hundreds of little things at precisely the right time in precisely the right way. On race day, your job is to run that program, not to chase positions or lap times.

Sports psychology splits sports performance into two mindsets: performing vs. executing. Executing means your attention is on the next brake point, your trail-braking, hitting the apex, keeping your eyes up the road...the stuff you’ve already made automatic. You know, the “flow state”.

I call it “running the program”.

Performing, however, means your attention is on the result: running position, lap times, expectations. I call it “outcome” mode. When you’re in outcome mode, your lap times suffer and mistakes pile up fast. And you get a worse result than you were capable of because you’re no longer running the program - you’re trying to “will” an outcome to happen.

I built The Racer’s Mind because my own discipline sometimes falls apart when a race gets tense and really matters to me. It’s really hard to catch yourself when you slip into “outcome” mode in real-time.

The Racer’s Mind was written to look for clues that you may have slipped into outcome mode and use verbal cues to snap you back into running the program. How does it do this? The Racer’s Mind connects to iRacing and heart rate monitoring to detect situations that are likely to pull you out of running the program and gently guide you back on track (pun intended).

What triggers The Racer’s Mind to intervene?

  • You go off or have an incident. Once the car has settled, it gives you a reset cue so the mistake stays behind you.
  • A car is right behind you. If a car behind is breathing down your neck and you start driving in your mirror or worrying about losing a position.
  • You’re closing on the car ahead. You’re gaining on the car ahead and getting excited about a pass.
  • You’re pushing past your practice. The Racer’s Mind learns how you drive during practice to set up a baseline. If you start pushing beyond how you practiced, it’ll gently tell you to back off and have patience.
  • Your heart rate stays high (optional). With a smartwatch and Pulsoid, The Racer’s Mind learns how your HR reacts during a race and if it’s elevated too high, it’ll get you to calm back down.
  • The race start and other special situations. Helpful reminders at race starts to prevent mistakes and nip the red mist in the bud.

Verbal cues are short and calm, in natural voices, and the racecraft cues go quiet in the closing minutes. Each one can be switched off. And you can record your own voices and cues for each trigger.

The idea

Hot-lapping is a horribly inefficient way to get faster. You’ll improve eventually, but by accident, and you’ll never reach your full potential.

It’s like learning guitar. You get better by doing scales and finger exercises, not by copying one song over and over. Sim racing is the same. Instead of building rote muscle memory for one car and track, you work on repeatable principles and techniques, then apply them to whatever combo you’re practicing for.

That’s deliberate practice:

  1. Set a baseline. Put down some honest, consistent laps.
  2. Review your fastest laps. Use the replay to judge things like track usage, and telemetry to look at braking technique and the timing between your steering and pedals. Use the two together to find clues like the wrong apex, or entering a corner too fast or too slow.
  3. Pick what matters most. Not everything is worth fixing first.
  4. Practice it on purpose. Build focused drills around those techniques and work on them, one at a time.
  5. Track progress. You can see how repeated sessions at the same combo improve after practice, so you know that you were effective.

How ThePerfectLap helps

  • Review your laps quickly. Bring your telemetry and your own observations together, corner by corner, so finding the flaws doesn’t take all night.

  • See if it’s working. Compare against earlier sessions at the same car and track.
